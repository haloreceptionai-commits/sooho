Rail ends almost touch, yet the gap saves trains. Steel rails get longer when hot and shorter when cold. Over a kilometer, a ten-degree rise adds about a finger’s width. So why do tracks have gaps, and why do some look gapless? Old lines used jointed rails: two ends bolted with bars, leaving a small space. In winter that space grows; in summer it nearly closes. Without room, heat makes rails shove sideways and buckle. The clickety-clack noise came from those joints, not as the goal. Today many mainlines weld rails into long continuous lengths. They set the rail at a neutral temperature so forces balance. Clips, anchors, and packed stone hold that stress in place. Still, insulated joints add small gaps where signals need them. In deep cold, joints can pull apart and break bolts. In extreme heat, welded rails can kink if not managed. Standards tell crews how to control these risks. The answer: give steel space or controlled grip so it can move safely. Like deck boards need gaps, but steel pushes harder, so we plan it. That prevents buckles and breaks, and explains the old clickety-clack. Modern mainlines often have no visible gaps at all.
